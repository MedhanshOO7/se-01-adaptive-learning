# Atomic Foundations of Matter — content notes

Original teaching notes based on [NCERT chapter 9](sources.md), with illustrative examples. It follows the 2026 book's sections, not the older book's chapter numbers.

## 9.1 Conservation of mass

In an ordinary chemical reaction, total mass of the reactants equals total mass of the products when **all material is accounted for**. A reaction rearranges atoms; it does not create or destroy atoms of each element. A physical change also conserves mass: dissolving salt in water does not make the salt disappear.

In an open reaction producing gas, a balance can show a lower mass because some products leave the measured system. Capture the gas and include its container in the measurement to test the entire system. The chapter compares open and balloon-closed vinegar and baking-soda setups and includes a closed precipitation experiment. The outcome is an inference supported by careful measurement, not a claim that the mass of liquid alone always stays unchanged. Conduct chemical demonstrations only under teacher supervision, with suitable apparatus and local safety procedures.

**Worked example:** A closed vessel contains 12 g of reactants and has 7 g of one product. If there are only two products, the other has mass 5 g. A claim about an open vessel needs information about any escaping material.

## 9.2 Constant proportions

A pure chemical compound contains the same constituent elements in the same fixed **mass ratio**, regardless of its sample's origin. Water's hydrogen:oxygen atom ratio is 2:1, but with approximate masses H = 1 and O = 16 its mass ratio is 2:16, or **1:8**. A water sample containing 3 g hydrogen contains 24 g oxygen. Mass ratio and number-of-atoms ratio are different. Mixtures do not have a fixed composition. The chapter uses cinnabar, mercury sulfide, as a historical and Indian-context example.

## 9.3 Dalton's atomic theory

Dalton explained chemical combinations using atoms: matter consists of atoms; atoms of an element have characteristic properties; atoms of different elements differ; compounds form from atoms in fixed simple whole-number ratios; and reactions rearrange atoms. These ideas help explain conservation and fixed proportions. His historical assumption that atoms are indivisible and all atoms of an element have the same mass is revised by subatomic particles and isotopes. Nuclear reactions require a different treatment from ordinary chemical reactions.

## 9.4 How atoms combine

An **atom** is a basic unit of an element. A **molecule** is a discrete neutral group of bonded atoms: O₂ is a molecule of an element, H₂O a molecule of a compound. Atoms of an element may occur as individual atoms or in other structures; not all substances consist of discrete molecules. **Atomicity** counts atoms in one molecule: O₂ has 2, O₃ has 3, P₄ has 4. A compound contains two or more elements chemically combined in a definite composition.

### Covalent bonding: sharing

Atoms can share electron pairs; the shared pair forms a covalent bond. H₂ has one shared pair; HCl contains H and Cl, H₂O has two O–H bonds, and CH₄ has four C–H bonds in the usual elementary diagrams. O₂ and N₂ are represented by double and triple bonds respectively. Electron-dot diagrams represent valence electrons and shared pairs, and are models, not pictures of precise electron paths. Shared electrons can help atoms reach a full outer configuration, but the octet rule is an introductory guide with exceptions.

### Ionic bonding: attraction between ions

An atom that loses electrons forms a positive **cation**; one that gains electrons forms a negative **anion**. For sodium chloride, the elementary electron-transfer model shows Na → Na⁺ + e⁻ and Cl + e⁻ → Cl⁻. Oppositely charged ions attract. In solid NaCl, many ions form an extended lattice; NaCl gives the smallest whole-number ratio of Na⁺ to Cl⁻, rather than a separate NaCl molecule. For MgCl₂, one Mg²⁺ balances two Cl⁻ ions. Charge comes from the difference between proton and electron counts; changing electrons does not change the element.

**Polyatomic ions** are charged groups of atoms. The group behaves as an ion in formula writing; the bonds within the group can be covalent. Common examples: ammonium NH₄⁺, hydroxide OH⁻, nitrate NO₃⁻, hydrogencarbonate HCO₃⁻, carbonate CO₃²⁻ and sulfate SO₄²⁻.

## 9.5 Naming and writing formulae

Read subscripts as numbers of atoms or ion groups, not charges: H₂O contains two H atoms and one O atom; Ca(OH)₂ has one Ca, two O and two H atoms. A coefficient multiplies a whole formula: 2H₂O represents two water molecules, four H atoms and two O atoms.

For simple ionic formulae, write the cation then the anion, choose the lowest whole-number ratio that gives zero net charge, and use parentheses when more than one polyatomic ion is required. Examples:

| Ions | Balance of charge | Formula | Name |
|---|---|---|---|
| Al³⁺, O²⁻ | 2 × (+3) + 3 × (−2) = 0 | Al₂O₃ | aluminium oxide |
| Ca²⁺, Cl⁻ | +2 + 2 × (−1) = 0 | CaCl₂ | calcium chloride |
| Ca²⁺, OH⁻ | +2 + 2 × (−1) = 0 | Ca(OH)₂ | calcium hydroxide |
| NH₄⁺, SO₄²⁻ | 2 × (+1) − 2 = 0 | (NH₄)₂SO₄ | ammonium sulfate |
| Mg²⁺, O²⁻ | +2 − 2 = 0 | MgO | magnesium oxide |

A criss-cross shortcut works for simple ionic charges only if the ratio is simplified and groups remain intact. Variable-charge metals require their stated ion: FeCl₂ is iron(II) chloride and FeCl₃ iron(III) chloride; the chapter also uses ferrous/ferric names. A covalent substance's molecular formula represents an actual composition, so do not automatically reduce every molecular formula as if it were an ionic ratio: H₂O₂ is distinct from HO.

## 9.6 Properties and tests

Typical ionic compounds have strong attractions in their lattices and often have high melting points. They conduct when molten or dissolved **if mobile ions are present**, but not as solid crystals where ions cannot move freely. Many ionic compounds dissolve in water, though solubility varies. Many simple molecular covalent substances have relatively low melting and boiling points and do not conduct electricity; giant covalent structures are important exceptions, so these are trends rather than universal rules. Water solubility varies for both categories. The textbook includes a solubility and electrical-conductivity comparison; interpret results based on the substance and conditions tested.

## 9.7–9.8 Molecular mass and formula unit mass

Using approximate atomic masses in unified atomic mass units (u), add the mass contributions of every atom indicated by a formula. **Molecular mass** is for a discrete molecule; **formula unit mass** describes an ionic formula unit.

- H₂O: 2 × 1 + 16 = **18 u** per molecule.
- CO₂: 12 + 2 × 16 = **44 u** per molecule.
- NaCl: 23 + 35.5 = **58.5 u** per formula unit.
- Ca(OH)₂: 40 + 2 × (16 + 1) = **74 u** per formula unit.

These examples use rounded average atomic masses; isotope-specific molecules can have different masses. A formula's subscripts are counts, not mass ratios, and 18 u per water molecule is not 18 g. The mole and molar mass are separate concepts.
