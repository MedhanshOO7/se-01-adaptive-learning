# Theoretical foundations: Atoms and Molecules

This is a **backwards map of scientific ideas**. Start with an idea in the Class 9 chapter and repeatedly ask: **“What underlying theory explains this? What explains that?”** The branches trace conceptual explanations, not procedures, formula-reading skills, arithmetic, question difficulty or a lesson sequence.

The first layer is the chapter claim. Each step below it goes deeper into the ideas used to explain that claim. Different branches can go in different directions; depth is relative to the idea where we start. This is a proposed research map, not an official NCERT taxonomy or a complete theory of chemistry. The boundary between school-level models and later scientific theories is marked explicitly. Some later theories describe a broader framework rather than a simple proof of the layer above.

## 1. Why does mass stay the same in a chemical reaction?

**Chapter idea:** In an ordinary chemical reaction, total reactant mass equals total product mass when the whole system is included.

```text
Mass is conserved in an ordinary chemical reaction
└── A chemical reaction rearranges atoms into different substances.
    └── In ordinary chemical reactions, atoms of each element are conserved;
        chemical bonds change, but the atomic nuclei remain unchanged.
        └── An atom's mass is concentrated in its nucleus, which contains
            protons and neutrons; its electrons have much smaller mass.
            └── A nucleus is a bound system of nucleons; its mass also
                reflects nuclear binding energy. [beyond chapter]
```

At chapter level, atoms being rearranged is the useful explanation. For higher precision, chemical binding energy changes also contribute an extremely small mass difference through mass–energy equivalence; the school conservation law is an excellent approximation for ordinary reactions. Nuclear reactions are a different context and should not be explained by claiming nuclei always remain unchanged.

**Related distinction:** An open container can appear to lose mass when a product gas escapes. That is a question about what is included in the measured system; it does not explain the fundamental conservation principle.

## 2. Why does a compound have constant mass proportions?

**Chapter idea:** The elements in a given pure compound occur in a fixed ratio by mass.

```text
A given compound has a fixed elemental mass ratio
└── Its constituent elements occur in a characteristic composition.
    ├── In a molecular compound, each molecule of a given substance
    │   contains a characteristic number of atoms of each element.
    └── In a simple ionic compound, the solid has a characteristic
        whole-number ratio of its component ions.
        └── A stable bulk ionic compound is electrically neutral:
            total positive and negative charges balance.
```

A second branch explains the conversion from composition to mass ratio:

```text
Fixed ratio of numbers of atoms → fixed mass ratio
└── Atoms of different elements have different characteristic masses.
    └── Atomic masses depend mainly on their protons and neutrons;
        naturally occurring isotopes contribute to average atomic mass.
```

Water has two H atoms for each O atom; because their masses differ, its hydrogen-to-oxygen mass ratio is approximately 1:8, not 2:1. This is the introductory law for a given compound; isotope variations and non-stoichiometric solids are later qualifications, not reasons to discard the school-level idea.

## 3. Why do atoms form covalent molecules?

**Chapter idea:** Atoms can form covalent bonds by sharing electron pairs.

```text
Some atoms form a covalent molecule
└── A bonded arrangement can have lower total energy than separated atoms.
    └── Electron–nucleus attractions and electron–electron and
        nucleus–nucleus repulsions together determine the energy.
        └── Electrons in atoms and molecules are described by quantum states;
            a molecular electronic state can extend over more than one nucleus.
            [beyond chapter]
```

**Chapter model:** A shared electron pair represents a covalent bond. Outer-shell arrangements and duet/octet patterns help explain familiar examples such as H₂, H₂O and CH₄. They are useful patterns, not a universal cause or a complete energy explanation. A molecule is a discrete entity; a giant covalent structure is not a collection of separate simple molecules.

## 4. Why do ions and ionic compounds form?

```text
An ionic solid contains positive and negative ions
├── A cation has fewer electrons than the total positive charge of its
│   nucleus; an anion has more electrons than its proton count.
│   └── Electron loss or gain changes net charge without changing the
│       number of protons or the element's identity.
└── Oppositely charged ions attract electrically.
    └── Electrostatic attraction is an interaction between electric charges.
        └── In an ionic solid many ions form a lattice; attraction and
            repulsion together contribute to the structure's energy.
            [lattice-energy treatment beyond chapter]
```

The electron-transfer picture is a model of the change in electron ownership in familiar ionic compounds. Forming a compound is not explained by electron transfer alone: the overall energy change, including the attraction and arrangement of ions, matters. NaCl represents a repeating 1:1 ratio in a lattice, rather than isolated NaCl molecules in the solid.

## 5. Why do ionic compounds have definite ratios?

```text
A neutral sample of a simple ionic compound has a definite ion ratio
└── The total positive charge and total negative charge balance.
    ├── Ions carry characteristic charges in the compound under discussion.
    └── Electric charge is additive: the contributions of all ions combine.
        └── Net charge depends on the balance of positive and negative charge.
```

For aluminium oxide, Al³⁺ and O²⁻ balance in a 2:3 ratio. The formula Al₂O₃ is a representation of that underlying relationship. The formula-writing technique itself is not a deeper theory. Polyatomic ions add another layer: atoms are bonded within a charged group, while the group participates as an ion in the ionic arrangement.

## 6. Why do substances conduct electricity differently?

```text
Molten NaCl conducts electricity; solid NaCl ordinarily does not
└── Electric current requires charge carriers that can move through the material.
    ├── Both solid and molten NaCl contain charged ions.
    └── Ions are constrained in the solid lattice but can move in the melt.
        └── The state and structure of a material affect particle mobility.
```

A salt solution can conduct when it contains mobile ions. Many simple molecular covalent substances lack mobile charged particles and conduct poorly; this does not establish a universal rule for all covalent materials. The existence of charged particles and their ability to move are distinct ideas.

## 7. Why do molecular and formula-unit masses have their values?

```text
A molecule's mass is approximately the sum of its constituent atomic masses
└── A molecule contains a definite collection of atoms of particular elements.
    ├── Atoms of different elements have different masses.
    │   └── Their masses primarily reflect their nuclear composition.
    └── The mass of a collection reflects the contributions of its constituents.
        └── Binding energy also affects the exact mass. [beyond chapter]
```

For an ionic solid, a **formula-unit mass** uses the component atoms in the simplest ion ratio; it does not imply that a discrete molecule exists. Textbook calculations usually use average atomic masses, so an isotopically specific molecule need not have exactly the tabulated average mass. Counting symbols and multiplying are methods used to apply this theory; they are not deeper explanatory layers.

## Shared theoretical foundations

| Foundation | Ideas it helps explain | Further theory below it |
|---|---|---|
| Matter has atomic structure | Composition, reactions, molecular mass | Subatomic particles and nuclear structure, chapter 8 |
| Chemical change rearranges atoms | Conservation of mass and Dalton's model | Bonds involve electrons; nuclei generally remain unchanged in chemical reactions |
| Elements have characteristic atomic identities and masses | Fixed proportions and relative masses | Proton number defines identity; neutron differences produce isotopes |
| Electric charge and electrostatic interaction | Ion formation, ionic bonding, conductivity | Charged particles exert electrical forces; mobility affects current |
| Energy and stability of arrangements | Why bonding can occur and lattices form | Attractions, repulsions and quantum descriptions of electronic states |
| Definite composition and structure | Compounds, molecules and formula units | Discrete molecular structure or characteristic extended-lattice ratio |

- An arrow means the lower statement is a proposed conceptual foundation or explanation of the statement above. Parallel branches are complementary; they are not always a single strict chain.
