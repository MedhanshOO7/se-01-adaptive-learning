# Chapter content: Journey Inside the Atom

This is an original account of [NCERT chapter 8](https://ncert.nic.in/textbook/pdf/iesc108.pdf), organised in its teaching sequence. For assessment scope and edition see [sources](sources.md).

## 8.1 The roots of atomic ideas

Living and non-living matter is made of atoms. Ancient Indian thinker Acharya Kanada proposed extremely small indivisible *parmanu*, whose combinations produce material things. Greek thinkers Leucippus and Democritus also suggested indivisible particles, called *atomos*. These early proposals were philosophical rather than experimental findings. Dalton's 1808 atomic theory developed a scientific account based on the experiments available at the time, treating atoms as indivisible. Later evidence showed atoms contain smaller particles. This is a story of models changing with evidence, not a claim that the ancient ideas were the modern experimental model.

## 8.2 The historical models

### Electrons and Thomson

In cathode-ray experiments J. J. Thomson inferred tiny negatively charged particles, now called electrons. The rays had the same character when the gas or electrode material changed, supporting the idea that electrons are present in atoms of different elements. Since ordinary atoms are neutral, the negative charge implied a balancing positive component. Thomson proposed electrons embedded in a positively charged sphere. His model could account for overall neutrality but did not contain a concentrated nucleus.

### Rutherford and the gold foil

A beam of positively charged alpha particles was directed at very thin gold foil. Most passed through, some changed direction and a very small fraction rebounded. Most passing through supports an atom consisting largely of space; rare strong deflections support a very small, concentrated, positively charged centre. Rutherford's nuclear model places nearly all the mass in that nucleus, with electrons around it. Deflection is evidence for a dense positive centre; it does not directly demonstrate electron orbits or discover neutrons.

A typical atomic diameter is roughly 10⁻¹⁰ m; a nuclear diameter is roughly 10⁻¹⁵ m. These are order-of-magnitude comparisons, not exact universal sizes. Under classical physics an orbiting charged electron accelerates and should radiate energy; the electron would spiral inward. Rutherford's model therefore could not explain atomic stability. Rutherford identified the proton, the positively charged nuclear particle.

### Bohr and energy levels

In Bohr's 1913 model electrons occupy allowed stationary energy levels, labelled K, L, M, N or n = 1, 2, 3, 4. An electron in an allowed state does not radiate energy merely by being there. A transition to a higher level requires energy absorption; a transition to a lower level releases an energy amount equal to the difference. This historical model explains stability through its postulates. It is a teaching model: modern quantum theory describes probability distributions rather than exact miniature planetary tracks.

## 8.3 Mass and the neutron

The nucleus contains protons and neutrons. Chadwick discovered the electrically neutral neutron in 1932. Protons and neutrons each have approximately one atomic mass unit of mass, whereas an electron is much lighter; most atomic mass lies in the nucleus. Ordinary hydrogen-1 has no neutron, but other hydrogen isotopes do. Nuclear binding is more complex than a simple spacing analogy: the strong nuclear interaction binds nucleons, while proton electric repulsion also matters. The chapter notes nuclear science and the Bhabha Atomic Research Centre.

| Particle | Symbol | Relative charge | Approximate relative mass | Location in school model |
|---|---|---:|---:|---|
| Electron | e⁻ | −1 | about 1/1836 of a proton | outside nucleus |
| Proton | p⁺ | +1 | approximately 1 | nucleus |
| Neutron | n | 0 | approximately 1 | nucleus |

A neutral atom has equal proton and electron counts. A charged ion need not; use its charge to determine electron count. Element identity depends on proton count, not on electron or neutron count.

## 8.4 Symbols of elements

Dalton used pictorial symbols; Berzelius promoted letter-based notation. Element symbols are standardised through IUPAC. The first character is uppercase and an optional second character is lowercase: H, He, C, Cl. Some reflect Latin names, for example Na (natrium), K (kalium), Fe (ferrum), Cu (cuprum) and Au (aurum). Case matters: Co denotes cobalt; CO describes a combination of carbon and oxygen.

## 8.5–8.6 Atomic number, mass number and notation

**Atomic number Z = number of protons.** For a neutral atom only, electrons = Z. **Mass number A = protons + neutrons.** Thus neutrons = A − Z. Nuclear notation ᴬ_ZX combines an element symbol X with mass number A and atomic number Z. A is an integer count; average atomic mass is a different quantity in u.

Example: neutral ²⁷₁₃Al has 13 protons, 14 neutrons and 13 electrons. A ²⁷₁₃Al³⁺ ion has the same nucleus but 10 electrons. The ion example is an author-added extension to clarify neutrality, rather than an assertion that this exact calculation is required by the chapter.

## 8.7 Distribution of electrons

In the school shell model, shell n has theoretical maximum capacity 2n²: K 2, L 8, M 18, N 32. For the chapter's first eighteen neutral elements use the familiar distributions through 2,8,8; a shell's maximum capacity is not a rule that all its places fill before the next shell for all elements. Electron configuration records the number in each occupied shell. The outermost occupied shell is the valence shell, and its electrons are valence electrons.

## 8.8 Valency

Valency is combining capacity. In simple cases it can be understood through electrons lost, gained or shared to obtain a stable outer configuration. Sodium (2,8,1) commonly loses one, valency 1; oxygen (2,6) commonly gains or shares two, valency 2; carbon (2,4) commonly shares four, valency 4. Neon has a full outer shell and common valency 0; helium's first shell is full with two. This is a useful elementary rule, not a universal predictor of every compound or every element's valency. Sharing and electron transfer are different mechanisms; do not teach that carbon routinely gains or loses four electrons.

## 8.9 Isotopes, average mass and isobars

**Isotopes** have the same Z and different neutron numbers and therefore different A. Hydrogen-1, hydrogen-2 and hydrogen-3 all have one proton; they have zero, one and two neutrons respectively. Carbon-12, carbon-13 and carbon-14 have six, seven and eight neutrons. Neutral isotopes of an element have the same electron count and broadly similar chemical behaviour, though isotope effects exist. Physical and nuclear properties can differ.

The chapter cites uranium-235 as a nuclear fuel, cobalt-60 in radiotherapy, iodine-131 in some thyroid treatments, and carbon-14 in dating suitable once-living materials. These are contexts, not medical guidance; carbon dating is not suitable for every fossil or age.

A natural sample can contain several isotopes. Its average mass reflects their proportions: with a simplified 75% chlorine-35 and 25% chlorine-37, average = 0.75 × 35 u + 0.25 × 37 u = **35.5 u**. This does not imply a single atom has a mass number of 35.5. The mass numbers here approximate isotope masses; high-precision atomic weights use measured isotope masses and abundances.

**Isobars** have the same A but different Z, such as argon-40, potassium-40 and calcium-40. They are different elements. Isotopes and isobars answer different comparison questions.

The chapter ends by introducing the modern electron-cloud picture and methods that reveal atomic-scale structure. A scanning tunnelling microscope probes surfaces using tunnelling; it is not an electron microscope, despite an imprecise grouping in the textbook. The modern model replaces definite Bohr paths with probabilities.
